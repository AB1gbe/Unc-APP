# Craigslist Study

The owner asked: "do a study on craigslist and tell me what they do, business model, how they are successful, the marketing methods used etc".

> **How sure are these numbers?** Craigslist is private and publishes no accounts. Revenue and traffic figures are **estimates**, mostly from the AIM Group (a classifieds research firm), repeated by other sites. Treat them as "about right", not exact. Items marked *(known history)* come from widely reported events, not from this session's searches.

## 1. What Craigslist is
- A **classified-ads website**: one plain page per city with sections for jobs, housing, for sale, services, gigs, community and (once) personals. Anyone posts, anyone browses.
- **Started in 1995** by Craig Newmark as an **email list** of arts and tech events in San Francisco. People forwarded it and asked to add more things (jobs, apartments), so it grew into a website. It became a company in **1999**; Jim Buckmaster joined in 2000 and later became CEO.
- Spread city by city across the US, then to hundreds of cities in about 70 countries *(known history)*.
- The design has hardly changed since the 1990s: text links, blue and white, no photos on the home page.

## 2. Business model: "almost everything free, a few things paid"
| What | Price | Who pays |
|---|---|---|
| Most posts (for sale, housing by owners, community, services in most cities) | **Free** | — |
| **Job ads** | About **$10–$75** per ad, most in big cities (SF, LA, NYC) | Employers |
| Apartment ads by **brokers** in New York | Paid | Brokers |
| Car and truck ads by **dealers** | Paid | Dealers |
| Gigs, some services, furniture by dealers in some areas | Small fees | Businesses |

- About **99% of posts are free**. Money comes from **businesses** posting in a few sections; ordinary people almost never pay.
- **No banner ads, no selling user data, no venture capital.** Craig started by charging San Francisco employers a small fee for job ads, which paid for everything else.
- **Jobs are the biggest earner** (one estimate: about 36% of revenue).
- **Revenue (AIM estimates):** peak about **$1.04 billion (2018)**; about **$300 million (2024)**. Traffic is about a third of its 2017 peak.
- **Very few staff:** about **50 people**, so profit margins are estimated at **80–90%**. Little engineering, almost no sales or marketing team.
- Ownership *(known history)*: eBay bought a 28% stake in 2004; a long court fight followed, and Craigslist bought the stake back in 2015. Newmark and Buckmaster have always refused to sell or go public.

## 3. Why it succeeded
1. **First, and free, in each city.** It arrived before newspapers moved classifieds online, and charged nothing where newspapers charged per line. It took most of US newspaper classified revenue (often called "the Craigslist effect" on newspapers).
2. **Network effects per city.** Buyers go where sellers are and sellers go where buyers are. Once a city's page was the busiest, rivals couldn't catch up. They grew **city by city**, not all at once.
3. **Trust and a "people helping people" culture.** Newmark took the generous spirit of early online communities (Usenet, The WELL). The two rules he repeats: *treat people as you want to be treated* and *live and let live*.
4. **The community moderates.** Anyone can **flag** a post; enough flags hide it automatically. A tiny staff can run a huge site because members police it.
5. **Simple and fast.** No sign-up needed to browse, few clicks, loads on any connection, looks the same for 25+ years: people know exactly how to use it.
6. **Charging only those who profit.** Employers, brokers and dealers make money from their posts, so they pay; everyone else is free, which keeps the supply of listings huge.
7. **Privacy by design** *(known history)*: emails are relayed through anonymous addresses, so buyers and sellers don't see each other's email until they choose to.
8. **Patience over profit.** No investors pushing for growth, ads or a sale, so they kept the product the same and the costs low.

## 4. Marketing methods
- **None, in the usual sense.** Newmark said in 2004 the company had **never advertised**. Growth came almost entirely from **word of mouth**:
  - the original email list was forwarded friend to friend;
  - each successful sale or rental brought the next user ("I found my flat on Craigslist");
  - free posting meant sellers told buyers where to look, and the reverse.
- **Press and reputation:** a quirky, non-corporate founder story and the "anti-business" stance earned free news coverage for decades.
- **Search engines:** millions of plain-text listing pages were easy for Google to find, bringing free traffic.
- **The brand is the name and the habit**, not a campaign: "check Craigslist" became a verb-like habit in US cities.

## 5. Problems and decline
- **Scams and crime:** fake listings, payment scams, and some violent crime cases from meet-ups. Anonymous, free posting makes fraud cheap.
- **Legal pressure:** "adult services" closed in **2010** under pressure from US states; **personals** closed in the US in **March 2018** after the FOSTA law made sites liable for trafficking ads ("We can't take such risk without jeopardizing all our other services").
- **Facebook Marketplace (2016):** real names, photos, Messenger and billions of users. Craigslist's traffic lead fell from about **43×** Marketplace (July 2017) to about **5×** (June 2018).
- **"Unbundling":** investor Andrew Parker's famous "Spawn of Craigslist" picture showed startups taking one section each and doing it better: **Airbnb** (short stays), Zillow/Apartments.com (housing), Indeed (jobs), Thumbtack/TaskRabbit (services), OfferUp (local selling), Tinder (dating), Etsy and others. Together they raised billions of dollars.
  - *(Known history)* Airbnb even grew by **letting hosts re-post their listings to Craigslist** in one click, taking Craigslist's audience.
- **Old design:** no photos-first browsing, poor mobile experience for years, no ratings or profiles, so less trust than newer apps.
- **Jobs ads**, the main earner, fell most (−32% in one year) as LinkedIn and Indeed took over.

## 6. Lessons for Unc
| Craigslist did | What it means for Unc |
|---|---|
| Free for people, paid for businesses that earn from it | Keep **buyers free**. If Unc charges, charge **shops** (e.g. featured offers, more offers per month, a shop page), not people asking for help. Matches [[Siin Competitor Study]] thinking. |
| Won one city at a time | Win **one area of Bahrain** (e.g. Manama/Juffair) or one category (phones, car parts) before spreading. Empty pages kill a marketplace. |
| Word of mouth, no ads | Every closed deal should create the next user: "Found it" stories, invite credit (already built), shareable request links with previews (already built). |
| Community flagging | Unc already has reports and blocks. Adding "enough reports hide a request until checked" would let a small team cope. |
| Simple, fast, stable design | Don't keep redesigning. Once the look is settled, keep it so people learn it. |
| Anonymous email relay | Unc's chat-only contact and hidden numbers until a price is agreed are the same idea, and stronger. |
| **Weakness:** no identity, scams | Unc's verified shops (KYC), ratings and "only about a specific request" rule fix exactly what hurt Craigslist. Say this in marketing: "the safe Craigslist for Bahrain". |
| **Weakness:** general site beaten by specialists | Unc's reverse model (buyers post, shops come to them) is itself a specialised "unbundled" idea. Pick a few strong categories to lead with instead of all 16 equally. |
| **Weakness:** legal shocks closed whole sections | Keep risky categories out from the start and follow Bahrain's PDPL ([[Bahrain Market Research]]). |
| Tiny team, high margin | The Cloudflare test setup and in-app tools (Get Help, admin queue) keep running costs low. |
